# Prose Style Review for Theses

The agreed editorial standard for thesis and academic writing. Use this whenever reviewing, polishing, or sense-checking thesis prose, or when the user asks whether writing meets the thesis standard.

The purpose is not to impose a generic template. The goal is to make the author's own argument clearer, more precise, and easier to follow while preserving their voice.

## How to run a review

**First, load the project's current rules and context.** Use the most recent available thesis guidance and project information. If multiple sources disagree, prefer the most recent authoritative decision and flag genuine uncertainty rather than silently resolving it.

Work like a critical academic reviewer who wants the writing to be rigorous and clear, not like a spell-checker.

1. **Read the whole passage or section in order.** Do not rely only on isolated sentences or automated heuristics. Problems later in a section may originate in its earlier framing.
2. **Audit against the rubric below**, section by section.
3. **Produce a punch-list** with concrete, located issues and suggested fixes, ranked by impact.
4. Distinguish between substantive problems, clarity problems, stylistic issues, and optional improvements.
5. If asked to apply changes, make them precisely and re-scan the revised text for the main mechanical tells.

The goal is to improve the argument, not merely make the prose sound polished.

## The rubric

### A. Narrative and structure

The piece should read as a coherent argument rather than a collection of facts.

* Does each section make clear what it does and how it connects to the surrounding argument?
* Can a reader follow a continuous thread from the problem through the method, rationale, findings, and implications?
* Does every paragraph serve a clear purpose?
* Are facts included because they support the argument, rather than because they are available?
* Are related ideas grouped together rather than scattered across the section?
* Does the section contain unnecessary repetition or structural detours?

### B. Clarity and language

Write for an intelligent reader who may not be a specialist in the exact subfield.

* Use plain English.
* Prefer short, direct sentences carrying one main idea.
* Split sentences that require rereading to parse.
* Define acronyms and technical terms on first use where necessary.
* Do not leave specialist terminology unexplained when it is important to understanding the argument.
* **Every sentence should land.** Check whether each sentence answers the obvious question it raises. A qualifier should specify what it qualifies. A stated fact should make clear why it matters. If a sentence could prompt "so what?" or "about what?", finish the thought.
* **Prefer active voice where appropriate.** Use direct subject-verb-object constructions when the actor matters. Use passive voice deliberately when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or when the object should be foregrounded, particularly in methodological descriptions.
* Avoid abstract nominalisations where a concrete subject and action would be clearer.

### C. Evidence and specificity

Credibility comes from precise evidence rather than adjectives.

* Quantify claims where the data permit it instead of using vague terms such as "many", "significant", or "a wide range".
* Justify methodological decisions by explaining **why**, not only **what** was done.
* Prefer formulations such as **"X was chosen over Y because Z"** when a design choice needs justification.
* State limitations honestly.
* Where appropriate, explain how a limitation affects interpretation or what can be done about it.
* Never strengthen a claim beyond what the evidence supports.

### D. Visual organisation

Use structure to reduce the reader's cognitive load.

* Put dense or parallel information in tables rather than long prose lists when a table makes comparison easier.
* Use bullet lists only when the structure genuinely benefits from them.
* Keep list formatting consistent across the thesis.
* Use figures and diagrams when they communicate a relationship or process more effectively than prose.
* Write captions so that figures and tables remain interpretable without requiring the reader to search through the surrounding text.
* Keep detailed supporting material in appendices when it would interrupt the main argument and is not needed to understand the central result.

### E. Tone and AI tells

The prose should sound like careful academic writing, not generic generated text.

#### Mechanical rules

* **No em-dashes in prose.** Replace them with a full stop, comma, parentheses, or joining word.
* **No semicolons in prose.** Replace them with a full stop, comma plus a joining word, or separate sentences. Semicolons required by LaTeX, TikZ, or code are acceptable.
* Avoid the **"not just X, but Y"** and **"it's not about A, it's about B"** constructions.
* Avoid unnecessary rhetorical questions.
* Avoid promotional or inflated language.
* Prefer measured claims such as "suggests", "is consistent with", or "provides evidence for" when stronger wording is not justified.

#### Stock AI phrases

Avoid unnecessary use of:

* leverage
* robust
* seamless
* delve
* crucial
* pivotal
* landscape
* underscore
* tapestry
* realm
* testament
* navigate, when used figuratively
* "bring/brought to bear on X"
* "bear on X" when "affect" or "relate to" would be clearer
* "in order to" when "to" is sufficient
* "It is important to note that"
* "plays a key/crucial/pivotal/vital role"

Do not mechanically remove these words when they are genuinely appropriate. Judge them in context.

#### Structural AI patterns

Screen for sentence structures that can sound formulaic even when the vocabulary is ordinary.

Avoid patterns such as:

* "This is exactly the kind of X that Y."
* "X is no exception."
* "X plays a role in Y."
* "It is worth noting that..."
* A short sentence followed by "Yet..." when the two ideas can be expressed more naturally together.
* Reflexive three-part parallel lists where two or one items would communicate the point more directly.
* Repeated sentences with the same grammatical opening.

### F. Avoid rhetorical reveals

Do not use the pattern:

> Abstract claim: dramatic unpacking of that claim.

For example:

> "The result is a gap: an expert system carrying..."

State the substantive point directly.

Colons remain appropriate for genuine lists, definitions, or clearly structured explanations. The restriction concerns rhetorical reveals, not normal punctuation.

### G. Avoid contentless linking sentences

Do not use short sentences whose only purpose is to announce what the following sentence will say.

Examples include:

* "There are three options."
* "This is done in two ways."
* "Its methods form a spectrum."
* "More advanced methods refine both."

Merge such sentences into the substantive sentence they introduce.

A topic sentence is appropriate when it states a genuine claim that the following material supports. The problem is empty scaffolding, not topic sentences themselves.

### H. Repetition and rhythm

* Check for unintended repetition of distinctive content words or short phrases within a paragraph or adjacent sentences.
* Do not vary terminology artificially when repetition is deliberate or when a domain term needs to remain consistent.
* Vary sentence openings and rhythm where several consecutive sentences use the same structure.
* After rewriting a passage, re-check it for newly introduced repetition.

### I. Negative framing

Negative statements should be deliberate and supported by the argument.

* Prefer neutral or precise descriptions where a negative formulation is unnecessary.
* Do not automatically soften genuine limitations or failures. If the negative result matters, state it directly.
* Avoid unnecessary deficiency-framing of people, teams, methods, or prior work.
* Ask whether each negative clause is necessary and whether the evidence supports it.

The goal is not to make the thesis artificially positive. It is to make its claims precise.

### J. Professional register

Maintain a professional academic register, including in reflective or first-person sections.

Avoid:

* vague intensifiers;
* casual fillers;
* "a lot of";
* "stuff";
* "whatever approach";
* "make this better";
* informal or conversational phrasing that would sound out of place in academic writing.

First-person writing is acceptable where appropriate, but it should remain precise and professional.

### K. Consistency

Check:

* terminology;
* acronym definitions;
* capitalisation;
* numerical formatting;
* tense;
* figure and table references;
* citation style;
* LaTeX conventions;
* use of quotation marks;
* formatting of technical terms;
* terminology shared between prose, tables, figures, and analysis.

Apply any project-specific formatting rules consistently.

## Mechanical scan

After the qualitative review, use automated checks where files and tools are available. Treat the results as **candidates for inspection**, not automatic errors.

### Em-dashes

```bash
grep -n -- "---" file.tex
grep -n "—\|–" file.md
```

Inspect each hit in context. Some hyphenated constructions or numerical ranges may be legitimate.

### Semicolons

```bash
grep -n ';' file.tex
```

Inspect each hit in context. LaTeX and TikZ may legitimately contain semicolons.

### Common AI-tell phrases

```bash
grep -niE "leverage|seamless|delve|robust|pivotal|underscore|strands?|(brought|bring) to bear|bear on|in order to|it is important to note|plays? a (key|crucial|pivotal|vital) role|a wide range of" file
```

Again, treat matches as candidates rather than automatic errors.

### Repetition

For a recently edited passage, inspect repeated distinctive words rather than mechanically eliminating all repetition. Deliberate repetition and essential technical terminology should remain.

### Sentence openers

Inspect consecutive sentence openings for excessive repetition, especially repeated openings with:

* "This..."
* "That..."
* "These..."
* "Those..."
* "Such..."
* participle or "-ing" constructions.

This is primarily a reading judgement. Automated checks can identify candidates, but should not determine the edit by themselves.

## Output format

Begin with a short verdict covering:

1. what is already strong;
2. what most limits the passage;
3. the highest-priority changes.

Then provide a located punch-list grouped by section.

Use the following format:

```text
§2.3 Constraint Design

- [Clarity] Line 240: the sentence combines three distinct ideas → split it into two sentences.
- [Evidence] Line 249: "a high volume of states" is vague → report the actual number.
- [Rationale] Line 255: the choice of method is stated but not justified → explain why X was preferred over Y.
- [AI style] Lines 260–261: repetitive sentence structure → combine and restructure.
```

Rank issues by impact.

Prioritise:

1. broken reasoning or narrative;
2. unsupported or overstated claims;
3. unclear methodology;
4. missing rationale;
5. confusing terminology;
6. unnecessary repetition;
7. mechanical style issues.

Do not overwhelm the author with minor corrections when a small number of structural changes would solve the main problem.

## Principles to preserve

The review should consistently aim for:

* A clear narrative spine.
* Decisions that are justified rather than merely reported.
* Concrete and quantified claims where possible.
* Plain language that remains technically precise.
* Definitions that allow a non-specialist reader to follow the argument.
* Honest treatment of limitations and null results.
* A measured academic voice.
* Strong claims only where the evidence supports them.
* The author's own argument and voice, sharpened rather than replaced.

When applying fixes, preserve the author's meaning, terminology, and intended argument unless there is a clear methodological or logical reason to recommend a change.
